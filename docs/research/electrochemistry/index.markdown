  <h1>Electrochemistry</h1>

  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research-electrochemistry2.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Electrochemistry">

  <p>
    My master's research focused on electrochemical catalysts for the anode reaction
    in glucose fuel cells. Glucose is a promising fuel because it is renewable,
    easy to store, and biologically abundant. However, its electrochemical oxidation
    is kinetically slow because glucose has a complex molecular structure and involves
    multiple adsorption and oxidation steps on the catalyst surface.
  </p>

  <h2>Research Motivation</h2>

  <p>
    Many reported glucose oxidation catalysts work efficiently only in strongly
    alkaline electrolytes. Although alkaline media can accelerate the reaction,
    they are not ideal for practical or biomedical applications because they are
    corrosive and may damage electrodes, cell components, or biological environments.
    Therefore, my research aimed to study Pt-based catalysts that can promote
    glucose oxidation in neutral aqueous electrolytes.
  </p>

  <h2>Mechanistic Study</h2>

  <p>
    I first examined suitable electrolytes and simple catalysts using cyclic
    voltammetry. Sodium sulfate solution was found to be more suitable than
    phosphate-buffered saline for analyzing glucose oxidation, because phosphate
    species introduce additional hydrogen-related redox processes and increase the
    double-layer capacitance, which can obscure small glucose-oxidation signals.
  </p>

  <p>
    After catalyst screening, Pt was selected as the most practical base catalyst.
    To investigate the reaction mechanism, I used electrochemical quartz crystal
    microbalance measurements, which allow simultaneous observation of current and
    electrode mass change. These results suggested that glucose oxidation on Pt
    involves adsorption of glucose-derived intermediates, formation of Pt-OH species,
    and subsequent oxidation to gluconolactone or gluconic acid.
  </p>

  <h2>Catalyst Design</h2>

  <p>
    Based on the mechanistic picture, I studied two Pt-based catalyst strategies:
    Pt/C mixed with PtO2 and PtPd alloy catalysts. The Pt/C-PtO2 system was designed
    to assist oxidative surface processes, while PtPd alloy catalysts were introduced
    to modify the surface electronic structure and promote oxygen-containing
    adsorbate formation. These studies connected catalyst design with the elementary
    steps inferred from electrochemical measurements.
  </p>

  <h2>Outcome</h2>

  <p>
    The final study proposed two possible glucose oxidation pathways on Pt-based
    catalysts: direct oxidation and OHads-mediated oxidation. PtPd catalysts were
    further evaluated in glucose-air fuel cells, where improved fuel-cell performance
    was observed. This work provided both mechanistic insight and catalyst-design
    guidance for abiotic glucose fuel cells.
  </p>
