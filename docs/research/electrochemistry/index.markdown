  <h1>Electrochemistry</h1>

  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research-electrochemistry2.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Electrochemistry">

  <p>
    My master's research focused on D-glucose oxidation at Pt-based electrodes in neutral
    electrolytes. I studied the surface reactions behind the measured current and used that
    picture to compare Pt-Pd anode catalysts for glucose fuel cells.
  </p>

  <h2>Research Motivation</h2>

  <p>
    Neutral-electrolyte glucose fuel cells are of interest for low-power and potentially
    implantable devices. Their anode reaction is slow, however, because glucose adsorption,
    intermediate formation, and oxidation all occur over the catalyst surface. Pt is a stable
    abiotic catalyst, but the origin of several features in its voltammogram had not been
    fully resolved.
  </p>

  <h2>Mechanistic Study</h2>

  <p>
    I combined cyclic voltammetry with electrochemical quartz crystal microbalance (EQCM)
    measurements, so that changes in current could be compared with changes in mass at the
    electrode surface. The observations were consistent with two pathways. Both begin with
    glucose adsorption through oxidative dehydrogenation. Adsorbed glucose can then undergo
    direct oxidation over a broad potential range; above about 0.8 V versus RHE, adsorbed OH
    also contributes to an indirect oxidation pathway.
  </p>

  <h2>Catalyst Design</h2>

  <p>
    The proposed mechanism suggested a role for Pd, whose surface forms adsorbed OH more
    readily than Pt. I synthesized PtPd and Pt<sub>2</sub>Pd nanoparticles and compared their
    glucose-oxidation activity. The stronger response of PtPd at high potential was consistent
    with a larger contribution from OH-assisted oxidation.
  </p>

  <h2>Outcome</h2>

  <p>
    The two catalysts were then used as anodes in single-chamber glucose-air fuel cells.
    The PtPd cell reached a maximum power density of 27.6 μW cm<sup>−2</sup> and an
    open-circuit voltage of 0.616 V, compared with 9.84 μW cm<sup>−2</sup> for the
    Pt<sub>2</sub>Pd cell. These fuel-cell measurements were consistent with the activity
    difference observed electrochemically and with the proposed role of surface OH.
  </p>
